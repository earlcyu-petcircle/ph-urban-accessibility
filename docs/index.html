PH / URBAN ACCESSIBILITY / METRO MANILA / 2026

How much of everyday life is a short walk from home?

Pick any three Metro Manila barangays and compare them on foot: how long residents walk to food, the palengke, a pharmacy, a clinic, a hospital, a public school, open space and transit, how many of those nine are within 15 minutes, and how crowded they are once neighbours are counted. Every resident counts: all 14 million people of the 2024 census, placed in their buildings and walked along the street network. Three barangays I have lived in are loaded first, as one point of view; swap in your own.

THE MEASURES /

Five ways to read one walk

No single number says how walkable a place is, so the comparison keeps five measures side by side rather than folding them into one score.

  • 15-minute score (0-100). The share of the nine services with at least one within 15 minutes on foot (1,050 m), averaged over the barangay's residents. 100 means everyone has all nine.
  • Minutes to the nearest. The walk for the barangay's median resident to the nearest service of each kind, at 4.2 km/h, along streets.
  • Residents within 10 minutes. The share within the 10-minute minimum (700 m), about the walk three in four Metro Manila walking trips stay within.
  • Transit. The UN's test of convenient public transport (SDG 11.2.1): within 500 m of a bus or jeepney stop or route, or 1 km of an LRT or MRT station.
  • Supply against Metro Manila. How much service there is per resident once the neighbours who share it are counted (E2SFCA, 15-minute catchment). A clinic round the corner serves far more people in a dense street.

The nine services: food retail (supermarket, grocery, convenience store), the palengke, pharmacy, primary care, hospital, public elementary school, public high school, open space of 0.1 ha or more, and transit.

ONE POINT OF VIEW /

Three barangays I have lived in

I was born and raised in Barangay 136 in Tondo, spent two years of college in Loyola Heights near Ateneo, and live in Oranbo, Pasig, now. They are three very different pieces of the city: one crowded block, a university barangay of campus, strip and villages, and a small barangay around a newer mixed-use district.

No barangay wins outright

  • Barangay 136 puts the most within a short walk: three in four residents have all nine basics within 15 minutes. But it has the least supply per person of the three once neighbours are counted (a third of Metro Manila's food supply per resident), and its longest walks are to the palengke and open space.
  • Oranbo has the nearest open space and palengke, and more supply per resident than Metro Manila for most services, but nobody there is within 10 minutes of a public high school.
  • Loyola Heights trails on most basics, above all public schools (about 21 minutes for the median resident), and only 48% of its residents have convenient public transport.

What survives ten changes of assumption

Every resident is counted, so there is no sampling error to test. Instead a difference between two of the three counts as a finding only if it keeps its order under all ten checks: walking speed, the time threshold, equal walk-sheds instead of barangay lines, straight-line distance, coarser origins, each data source alone, Overture's confidence cut-off, a second population surface and the jeepney rule. 14 of 33 pairwise differences do:

  • Loyola Heights has a longer walk than both others to food, pharmacies and public elementary schools, and fewer basics within 15 minutes than Barangay 136.
  • Barangay 136 has the shortest walk to a public elementary school, and the longest to open space.
  • Most differences between Barangay 136 and Oranbo reverse on equal walk-sheds, so they say as much about the barangays' sizes as about the places.

Barangays you pick on the map have not been through these checks, so read small gaps between them loosely.

METHOD /

How it was measured

  • Place every resident. Each barangay's 2024 census count is spread over WorldPop's 100 m cells, then over the residential buildings in each cell by footprint area: 1,573,122 buildings carry all 14,001,751 people.
  • Map the services. Overture Maps places and OpenStreetMap, merged with duplicates grouped, and DepEd's list of public schools; services up to 2 km beyond Metro Manila count.
  • Walk the streets. Every walkable OpenStreetMap way, expressways out and footbridges in, at 4.2 km/h, close to the observed Metro Manila pace.
  • Summarise per resident. Every figure is weighted by people, never by area; a barangay's minutes are its median resident's.
  • Show it without showing homes. The map shows barangays and H3 hexagons (about 0.015 km²); hexagons with fewer than five buildings are left off.

LIMITATIONS /

What to hold loosely

Each is set out in full in the paper's limitations section.

  • Sari-sari stores are almost unmapped. Only 234 are named in all of Metro Manila's open data, so the walk to everyday food is too long everywhere, most of all in dense streets.
  • Mapped is not there. New shops are missed and closed ones kept; Overture also lists some hospital clinics and wards as hospitals.
  • No health registry. The DOH facility registry cannot be exported, so hospitals and clinics rest on open map data, and hospitals count one each rather than by beds.
  • Jeepney routes are thinly mapped (95 in the study area), so transit shares are floors.
  • The walk itself is not scored: heat, floods, crossings and sidewalks are not in the network.
  • Potential, not realised, access: nothing here says where people actually go.

Run it yourself

Needs Python 3.12, curl and tippecanoe, about 1.3 GB of downloads, and the 2024 census workbook for Metro Manila saved by hand as data-quality.md describes.

The code, notebooks and full paper are in the GitHub repository.

git clone https://github.com/earlcyu-petcircle/ph-urban-accessibility.git
cd ph-urban-accessibility
./run_all.sh            # collect, build, analyse, map
python3 src/04_build_map/serve_map.py
# this page at http://localhost:8000/
